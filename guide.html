Reference guide

The human-interest story formula

A structure for success and impact stories that holds up across most major institutional donors, built from how experienced field communicators actually write these pieces — and a breakdown of where USAID, the EU, and UN agencies each ask for something different. Use it as a checklist before you draft, not a script to fill in mechanically.

Length
600–1,200 words, captions included
Sentences
14–20 words on average
Quotes
About 10–20% of the text
Photos
3–5, each captioned and credited

Step 1 · Pick a shape

Two shapes, not one

Pick one per story and keep to it from headline to ending. The split is less about programme sector and more about how much the physical setting can carry the story versus how much privacy and framing matter.

Reportage style

Best for livelihoods, shelter, water, cash and infrastructure stories — where a reader can picture the place and that does most of the work.

Headline
A short, concrete image, 6–8 words: "Fixing the pump before the dry season."
Opening
Starts in the scene, present tense, no donor mention yet.
Donor credit
Named mid-story, inside a short context section, with the full funding chain.
Rhythm
Shorter, punchier sentences; frequent short ones for emphasis.
Ending
A quote, or an understated fact — no closing appeal.

Programme style

Best for protection, psychosocial support, legal aid and other sensitive subjects, where framing and privacy matter more than scenery.

Headline
A fuller descriptive line, 10–17 words, often split by a colon.
Opening
Names who, the problem, and the support together, with the organization and donor stated up front.
Donor credit
In the first paragraph, alongside the organization's name.
Rhythm
Slightly longer, smoother sentences.
Ending
Hope and a realistic limit, held together, then a short present-day image.

Step 2 · Follow the arc

The story arc

Every strong human-interest story moves through roughly the same beats. The middle beats carry the story; the rest frame it. It helps to picture the rhythm as close, close, close, then a step back to explain why it matters, then close again for the ending.

#BeatWhat it does
1HeadlineNames the change or the tension through a concrete image, not a theme.
2StandfirstThe whole story in miniature: who, where, the problem, what changed — 2 to 3 sentences.
3Opening scenePuts the reader in the room: place first, present tense, physical detail, the person doing something.
4The personPlain facts — age, origin, work — plus one line or trait in their own words.
5BeforeThe problem as they lived it, through specific episodes with dates and durations.
6Zoom outA bridge sentence that turns one person into many, then sourced, dated figures.
7What we didThe support as mechanics — what, who, how long, what equipment; partner and donor named in full.
8The changeShown through actions and results, confirmed by someone else noticing, anchored by the strongest quote.
9Programme frameDates, regions, partners and reach — why it matters beyond one person.
10EndingHope and limits held together; the last word goes to the person, not a slogan.

Step 3 · Write it in voice

Voice and craft rules

The voice is a calm, exact observer who trusts detail and the person's own words to carry the feeling, rather than adjectives that tell the reader how to feel.

The person acts; the organization enables

Write the subject as the one doing things. Your organization provided tools or training; the person used them.

"She queued at the registry three times and kept every receipt herself" — not "we helped her register."

One attribution verb

Use "says," present tense, for quotes. Save "explains," "recalls" or "admits" for when the meaning actually calls for it. Skip "shared," "exclaimed," "expressed."

"Every family pays the same," she says.

Numbers are sourced, dated, and human-scale

Every figure needs a source and a period. Put it on a human scale by comparing before and after, not just stating a percentage.

Before training, her hives gave 5 kilograms of honey a season. This year, the best gave 14.

State an honest limit

Every credible story admits what the support didn't fix. That restraint is what makes the rest of the claims believable.

"The tank holds two days of water. A third dry week would still empty it."

Let someone else confirm the change

A second voice — a neighbour, trainer, teacher or colleague — who notices the change lands harder than the subject saying so themselves.

"The traders who once haggled over his nets now order them in advance."

Concrete, visible detail

List what's physically there, in the order the eye meets it. Name equipment and places precisely instead of generically.

A solar-powered pump that lifts water from the borehole into a 10,000-litre tank on the hill.

Step 4 · Watch your wording

Words that undercut the story

These words tell the reader how to feel instead of showing them, and most carry a pity or hero framing underneath. Swap them for the specific detail that made them true in this case. This list lines up with what the Caption Generator's own wording scanner flags on every draft.

  • beneficiary
  • vulnerable
  • resilient
  • empower
  • voiceless
  • hero / rescue
  • hopeless / helpless
  • journey
  • game-changer
  • a testament to
  • inspiring
  • heartbreaking

Step 5 · Name it well

Headline patterns

Five patterns cover most stories. Each names a concrete thing or a change — never a theme.

  • Action and purpose"Fixing the pump before the dry season"
  • First milestone"A first wage at 52"
  • From and to"From mending nets to running the cooperative"
  • Duration and turn"Three years without papers: a father gets his children registered"
  • After the event"After the floods: rebuilding homes on higher ground"

Step 6 · Check before you send

Checklists

Gather in the field

  • Exact location: village, district or region — never just "a camp"
  • Five or more physical details of the scene
  • Age or age bracket, origin, family, previous work
  • One specific episode from before, with a date or duration
  • Before-and-after numbers: income, output, hours, distance, cost
  • A second voice who can confirm the change
  • Three to five verbatim quotes, with the original language noted
  • What hasn't changed, or what still worries them
  • Consent explained calmly and recorded; pseudonym agreed if needed

Before you submit

  • Headline names a concrete image or change, not a theme
  • A quote at least every 150–200 words; the best one isn't buried
  • Every statistic has a source and a period
  • Organization, partners and donor named in full once, then acronyms
  • The person acts; the organization enables
  • None of the words from the list above
  • At least one honest limit stated
  • Ending is quiet — a quote or a present-day image, not a slogan
  • Donor disclaimer and pseudonym note in place, if used

Step 7 · Know your donor

How this formula bends per donor

The arc above holds across almost every institutional donor. What changes is length, attribution wording, and how formally consent and dignity get enforced. Always confirm against your specific award's branding and visibility annex — these are general patterns, not a substitute for it.

United States · USAID / BHA

Tighter length, strict branding

  • Partner success stories typically run to a fixed outline with a character limit (around 5,000 characters) — shorter and more templated than the full arc above.
  • Every public material, success stories included, must carry the USAID identity at a size and prominence at least equal to your own branding.
  • Required attribution line: "made possible by the generous support of the American People."
  • A formal Branding Strategy and Marking Plan is negotiated per award before any materials go out — check it before you assume the generic wording above applies.
European Union · DG ECHO

Optional format, mandatory emblem

  • A human-interest story is one of several optional "external communication" actions — partners commit to delivering a set number from a published menu, not every item on it.
  • Two baseline obligations are mandatory on everything regardless: the EU humanitarian aid visual identity displayed on site, and a written or spoken acknowledgement of EU funding.
  • Larger, EU-facing campaigns need their own ECHO-approved communication plan and budget before the contract is signed.
  • Final reports must evidence each visibility action you ticked as delivered.
UN agencies & UN-Habitat / UNICEF

Shorter, timelier, voice-led

  • Typical length runs shorter than the arc above — often 400–800 words, sometimes under 600, plus captioned photos and ready-to-use social copy.
  • Stories are expected close to the date of the event they describe, not months later.
  • Donor and implementing partner are always named in full.
  • Increasingly governed by a formal ethics framework (see UNHCR, right) rather than a style guide alone.
UNHCR ethical communications (2024)

Consent as an ongoing process

  • Six principles anchor every piece: do no harm, dignified representation, challenging stereotypes, amplifying the person's own voice, informed consent, and accountability.
  • Consent is treated as a continuing conversation, not a one-time signature — people can withdraw it after the fact.
  • Images cannot be used without consent, especially for fundraising, even if the story itself is otherwise cleared.

The baseline underneath all of them

Most major donors now expect NGOs to already follow a dignity-first code regardless of who is funding the work — most visibly the Dóchas Code of Conduct on Images and Messages, adopted across the European NGO sector. Its core commitments map directly onto the arc above: represent people in their wider context rather than an isolated image of need, obtain real understanding and permission before publishing, let people tell their own story in their own words, and record whether they want to be named at all.

In practice: get consent every time, offer a pseudonym whenever protection is a concern, and never treat a signed form as the end of the conversation — check back in before the story goes out publicly.
